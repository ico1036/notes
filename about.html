Jiwoong Kim

Particle physicist turned quantitative researcher, in Seoul. I build research systems that improve their own process, and then try hard to establish that the improvement is real.

The same problem twice

My doctorate was in particle physics, at Kyungpook National University, working on LHC data. The job, stated plainly, was to extract a signal from data that is overwhelmingly noise — and to be certain you had not manufactured it. I ended up arguing for convolutional networks on a classification problem the collaboration had always solved with physicist-designed variables, which improved signal efficiency by a factor of 1.85 and required training distributed across a thousand compute nodes to be tractable.

I now do quantitative research, and it took me a while to notice it is the same problem. A market is mostly noise. The effects worth having are small, they decay once known, and the failure mode is not missing them — it is convincing yourself of one that was never there. Physics has decades of apparatus built against exactly that: blind analysis, pre-registration, look-elsewhere corrections. Finance mostly does not, and reinvents it badly, one researcher at a time.

Both fields, in other words, are less about finding the answer than about building the machinery that stops you believing a wrong one. Which is what I actually work on.

Why recursive self-improvement

A direct attack on a hard problem buys you one answer. Building the loop that produces answers buys you every subsequent one, at falling cost — and if the loop also improves the process by which it improves, the returns compound instead of accumulating. That is the whole of it, and I think it generalises much further than the domain I happen to work in.

The uncomfortable half is that a loop like this is extremely good at persuading you it is working. It generates its own evidence, writes its own explanations, and grades itself on a number it can see. So most of the engineering is not the loop. It is the machinery that makes the loop's claims checkable by someone who does not believe them — withheld scores, predictions registered before results, tests that can only fail. The notes on this site are almost entirely about that half.

It is also testable, which matters more than the argument. Stripping the accumulated procedure off the same model and re-running its judgment blind against real outcomes moves the score from roughly chance to well above it, at p = 0.004. That measurement is in the second note, with what it does and does not establish.

Now

I lead a small team building a system that automates the research cycle end to end — forming a hypothesis, writing the code that expresses it, running it against sixteen years of market data, and folding the result, including the failures, into what the next iteration starts from. It runs unattended at roughly a thousand experiments a day.

Before that I built and ran a live equity model, and a pipeline that generates portfolios across a range of styles rather than a single one. The full record, with the numbers, is on my résumé; this page is the reasoning rather than the résumé.
